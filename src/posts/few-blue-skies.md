## Plot summary 
Paloma Vistamontes lives in San Fermín, a fictional town in California’s Inland Empire. A massive company called Selva is rapidly taking over the town’s every open space with warehouses. (A selva is a dense tropical rainforest; the word typically describes tracts in the Amazon basin.) San Fermín’s mayor promises that Selva will provide jobs, tax revenue, and growth. 

But Paloma knows what she’s seeing. Her father, a Selva warehouse worker on strike, is coughing more every day. Paloma gets nosebleeds, and her high school cancels class regularly when the air quality is intolerable. The only person who understands the weight of Paloma’s concerns is her ex-boyfriend Julio. Her closest confidant since childhood, Julio hasn’t spoken to Paloma since his pa died last year. 

When Selva proposes a new warehouse next door to their school, Paloma and Julio know they need to work together to stop it.

## Author background
Carolina Ixta is a writer and teacher (Elias, 2026). Her debut novel _Shut Up, This Is Serious_ won the Pura Belpré Award (Ixta, 2026). A child of Mexican immigrants, Ixta grew up in Oakland, California, but she set _Few Blue Skies_ in the Inland Empire after spending time there with her godmother. 

## Critical evaluation
_Few Blue Skies_ is a timely and well-researched venture into corporate exploit and environmental injustice. That’s about as much as I can say without spoiling the plot, because the last hundred pages are the highlight of this book. Despite slightly choppy prose, I loved the novel for its <span class="spoiler">heartbreaking ending: the romances that fall apart, the friendship that fades, the toxic corporation/municipal government/mainstream media triad</span> that gets exactly what it wants.

<span class="spoiler">I loved the sad ending because it’s full of love and growth. Julio and Paloma separate, for the second and final time, but they understand each other, perhaps more than they ever did before. Paloma learns to stop covering her grief with anger; Julio learns to   speak his grief aloud. She forgives him for taking Selva’s prize money, and he forgives her for deserting their project.</span>

Teens aren’t the only ones growing in this novel. <span class="spoiler">Paloma’s parents stop fighting, too, and Julio’s sister stops beating herself up over her father’s death. Paloma’s father makes peace with Alejandra’s, even after he breaks their strike, costing his coworkers their jobs.</span> In letting go of their differences, these characters retain the energy that they need to survive the smog, to resist Selva’s expansion, and to love their people another day.

## Library program
_Julio’s garden._ When Paloma and Julio start speaking again, she discovers that he’s been planting seeds in abandoned lots around town. Talk with teens about the wildlife that grows in your area, then plant seeds together in small nursery pots. Let teens take them home right away, or let the seeds start at the library and invite teens to pick them up after a few weeks.

## Speed-round book talk
Paloma Vistamontes lives in San Fermín, a fictional Inland Empire town that’s getting taken over by Selva warehouses. The e-commerce conglomerate promises growth and profit, but San Fermín residents see smog, precarious employment, and lung disease. The author shows Paloma’s family and friends doing their best to love each other while making difficult decisions to survive. Plus, she’s written my favorite teen romance in this collection.

## Potential challenges
In terms of sex, drugs, profanity, and violence, _Few Blue Skies_ is one of the most ascetic titles in this collection. But if I’ve taken anything away from this class, it’s that teen librarians should be prepared for any item to be challenged. _Few Blue Skies_ does present a strong case against capitalist expansion, and this political stance may engender challenges.

## Reason for inclusion
I originally chose this title for its discussion of youth action and environmental justice. Now that I’ve read the book, I’m also glad to include it for its themes about relationships and community survival.
